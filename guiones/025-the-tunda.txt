In this town, when your mother calls you from the trees, you look at her FEET before you answer.

Something lives in the mangroves behind our houses. The old people call it the TUNDA. It can wear the face of anybody you love. It can do their voice. But it can never get one foot right. One foot is always WOOD. Carved like the stick Grandma uses to whip hot chocolate. So we check FEET. At the door. At dinner. My uncle checks his own every morning. Follow it, and it feeds you river shrimp until you forget your own house. One catch. Nobody can look for you. You have to look DOWN yourself.

My mom went to the city for work eight months ago. She calls on Sundays. I always say we're fine. She used to whistle us in for dinner. Worst whistle alive. Like a kettle giving up. I'm fourteen. I cook the rice. I walk my little brother Ciro to school. And lately I do my homework on the back steps, facing the trees. Every evening, until dark.

Last Thursday at sunset, Ciro dropped his spoon. "Mom's home," he said. And he ran out the back door, into the mangroves. He's six. He didn't look down. From the trees, her voice called his name. Then a whistle. A PERFECT one.

I ran after him with the rice pot still in my hand. I found them by the black water. He was holding her hand. She had Mom's braid. Mom's yellow dress. I called his name ten times. He didn't turn around. I beat that pot like a drum. A hundred times. That's what the old people do when somebody gets taken. She smiled with my mom's mouth. So I grabbed his arm and pulled. He pulled back harder. "Go home," he said. "Mom's here." And she held out her other hand to me.

I ALMOST took it. That's the part I never told anybody. "Ciro," I said. "I saw her three weeks ago. I looked at her feet the first night. I KNEW. I still sat on those steps every evening. Just to hear her say my name." My voice was shaking. "I didn't want to look either. But Mom can't WHISTLE. Look down."

He looked. One bare foot. One foot of dark carved wood. He let go. She didn't chase us. Mom's face just went still, like a puddle when the wind stops. Then she walked back into the trees. Step, knock. Step, knock. I carried Ciro home. On Sunday the phone rang. For the first time, I didn't say we were fine. I said, "Mom. Come HOME."

She got off the bus Tuesday. Ciro and I both looked down. Two muddy sandals. Then she tried to whistle. Kettle. Giving up. Best sound I ever heard. I still checked her feet twice at dinner.
