In this town, something takes ONE thing from your house every night, and leaves one thing you LOST years ago.

It comes at three a.m. You never see it. You just hear a drawer slide open, slow. It took our TV remote and left a sock I lost in second grade. The sock was still wet. It took Dad's car keys and left his retainer from high school. The Fitzgeralds lost a couch and got back a hamster. The hamster looked like it had seen things. The trade is always FAIR. Small for small. Big for big. And you don't get to pick.

My Nana isn't scared of it. She calls it "our night guest" and leaves a cup of tea by the pantry. By morning, the tea is gone. The cup never is. Nana used to call me Pidge. Since March, she calls me "the NICE GIRL." She lost my name somewhere, and she can't find it.

So I had an idea. If our night guest brings back lost things, it could bring back that. Dad says by summer, she might lose his name too. I just had to offer something big enough.

Night one, I left my bike by the pantry. In the morning, there was a tricycle I lost when I was four. Still muddy. Nana patted my hand. "Thank you, nice girl." Night two, I left my phone. I got Nana's reading glasses, missing since 2015. She put them on and read me the cereal box. Twice. Night three, I left everything. My savings jar, two hundred and twelve dollars. My trophies. The TV. In the morning, the kitchen was full. Forty-one umbrellas. Nine library books. Every mitten our family ever dropped. Nana clapped. "What a lovely sale. Do you work here?"

Night four, I left NOTHING. I sat at the kitchen table in the dark and waited. 2:59. The fridge stopped humming. 3:00. The pantry door opened by itself, one inch. The air went cold. Something was breathing in there, slow, like a drawer sliding. I didn't look. "I don't have anything left," I whispered. "So take something of mine. Anything. I won't even ask what. Just give her back one morning." The breathing stopped. The tea went down, like somebody sipping. The door clicked shut.

In the morning, the table was empty. First time ever. Nana shuffled in, squinting at the floor. "PIDGE," she said. "Why is there a tricycle in my kitchen?" I couldn't talk. She made pancakes. She beat me at cards, cheating. By dinner, I was "the nice girl" again.

There's a clean square in the dust on my shelf. I don't know what used to sit there. Now I know how Nana feels. It still takes one thing every night. But last night, for the first time, it WASHED the cup.
