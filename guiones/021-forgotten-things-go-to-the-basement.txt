In this world, everything you forget ends up in your basement.

Keys. Homework. The name of that one actor. You go downstairs and there it is, on a shelf with a paper tag. My dad's basement has four hundred passwords and one wedding anniversary. Mom knew exactly which shelf. Our dog forgot a tennis ball in 2019. He still visits it. But there's a catch. You can't carry somebody else's forgotten thing back upstairs. It only comes up when they remember it on their own.

My grandma lives two streets over. She calls me Dino. When I was seven, I wore a green dinosaur cape for a whole year. I'm fifteen now, and she still cuts the crusts off my sandwiches. I've wanted to tell her to stop since sixth grade. Lately her basement has been filling up. Her glasses. Her lemon cake recipe. The word "umbrella."

Last Sunday she smiled at me like I was the mailman. "Can I help you, young man?" I laughed. She didn't. So I went down to her basement. Fourteen steps. It was colder than it should have been. Somebody was sitting on the bottom step, facing the wall. Gray hoodie. My hoodie. It turned around. It was me. Same scab on my chin. And a paper tag on its wrist. ARLO.

So I tried to bring me back up. I showed her forty photos of us. She said, "What a handsome boy. His grandma must be proud." Then I taped my school ID to her fridge. By lunch it was on a basement shelf. Then I took the other me by the hand and pulled him upstairs. Step eleven. Step twelve. On step thirteen he went thin, like breath on a window. Then he was back at the bottom, facing the wall.

I sat down at her kitchen table. She put a plate in front of me. A sandwich, crusts cut off. "My grandson likes them like this," she said. "He's about this tall." Her hand stopped at the edge of the counter. She hadn't forgotten me. She forgot I grew up. So I quit trying to make her remember fifteen. I dug the green cape out of her hall closet and tied it on. It fit like a napkin. I walked into her kitchen, five foot ten, and I roared.

She stared at me for a long time. Then she laughed so hard she had to sit down. "Dino," she said. "You got tall." And under the floor, I heard it. Footsteps on the basement stairs. One. Two. All fourteen. The basement door swung open. Nobody there. The bottom step was empty.

Her basement is still full. I check it every Sunday. I'm never in it. But my own basement has one new thing on the shelf. A tag that says TELL GRANDMA ABOUT THE CRUSTS. It can stay down there.
